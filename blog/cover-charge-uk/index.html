Cover charges in the UK: do you have to pay?

A cover charge is a fixed fee per diner. When it's legal in the UK, whether you have to pay it, if it goes to staff, and how to split it fairly on a group bill.

A cover charge is a fixed fee per person, added to a restaurant bill for things like bread, table linen or simply sitting down. In the UK it's legal as long as you were told about it before you ordered, usually on the menu. If it was shown clearly, it's generally part of the price and you're expected to pay it. If it wasn't, you have good grounds to ask for it to be taken off.

A cover charge is not the same as a service charge, and it doesn't have to go to staff. That's the part most people don't realise.

This isn't legal advice, but here's how it works in practice.

What is a cover charge?

"Cover" is restaurant shorthand for one diner's place at the table. A cover charge is a flat amount for each person, whatever they order. You'll usually see it on the receipt as one of these:

  • "Cover charge × 4"
  • "Cover 4 @ £1.50"
  • "Couvert" or "Table charge"

Two recent London examples got a lot of press in December 2024. Harrods added a £1 per person cover charge in its restaurants and cafés, on top of a 12.5% service charge (Startups.co.uk, Restaurant Online). The London Steakhouse Company, Marco Pierre White's steakhouse, charges £1.50 a person, which its website says covers "the cost of laundering table linen/napkins and other consumables" (Time Out, 17 December 2024).

In bars and clubs, "cover charge" usually means something different: a fee to get in the door. This article is about the restaurant kind.

Is a cover charge legal in the UK?

Yes, if it's clear before you order. What matters is that you know the full price when you decide to eat there.

Since 6 April 2025, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has required traders to show mandatory charges up front. The CMA's price transparency guidance (CMA209) lists "mandatory cover or service charges at a restaurant" as examples of mandatory charges. It says mandatory charges should always be given in the "invitation to purchase" and, wherever they can reasonably be worked out, included in the total price. The guidance names a restaurant menu, including a digital menu you open with a QR code, as an invitation to purchase.

In plain English: a compulsory cover charge belongs on the menu, where you can see it before ordering. A cover charge that first appears on the bill is the kind of hidden extra the rules are meant to stop.

HMRC's VAT guidance makes a similar point about service charges: "it is the menu that sets the contract between the restaurant and the customer" (VATSC06130). A charge added to the bill afterwards comes too late to be part of what you agreed.

Do you have to pay a cover charge?

If it was on the menu: generally yes. It was part of the price when you ordered, in the same way as a compulsory service charge. That said, some restaurants will remove it if you ask. The London Steakhouse Company told Time Out: "if a customer objects, our policy is to remove the charge from their bill." There's no harm in asking politely.

If it wasn't shown anywhere before you ordered: ask for it to be removed. You can point out that you weren't told about it.

"We didn't see a cover charge on the menu. Could you take it off, please?"

If it's for something you didn't have, like bread you sent back, it's reasonable to ask:

"We didn't have the bread. Could you take the cover charge off for our table?"

Stay calm and polite. Staff often have no say in the policy, and a quiet word at the till usually works better than a debate at the table. If the manager refuses a charge that genuinely wasn't shown in advance, pay it, keep the receipt, and follow it up in writing afterwards.

Is a cover charge the same as a service charge?

No. The difference matters for your money and for the staff.

Cover charge Service charge
How it's worked out Flat amount per person Percentage of the bill, often 12.5%
Usually optional? No, it's normally part of the price Yes, most are discretionary
Must it go to staff? Not necessarily Yes, since October 2024

A discretionary service charge is optional and you can ask for it to be removed. Our guide on whether service charge is mandatory explains the difference.

Does a cover charge go to the staff?

Not necessarily. Since 1 October 2024, the Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023 has required employers to pass all tips, gratuities and service charges they control to workers. But a cover charge is usually treated by the business as part of the price, like the cost of the food. Startups.co.uk reported in December 2024 that cover charges "are not covered by the new laws" because they don't relate to the standard of service.

That's now being tested. In early 2026, 29 Harrods workers backed by the United Voices of the World union took a claim to an employment tribunal, arguing the £1 cover charge works like a service charge and should be shared with staff. The union called it the first legal challenge to test the definition of a tip under the 2023 Act. Harrods denies the claims and says the cover charge is separate from its discretionary service charge (Restaurant Online, 2 March 2026). At the time of writing (November 2026) we couldn't find a published ruling.

So if you want to be sure your tip reaches the people who served you, treat the cover charge as part of the price and tip separately (or pay the service charge). More on that in who gets the tip?

Is service charge added on top of the cover charge?

It depends on the restaurant. Some work out the 12.5% on everything, cover charge included. Others leave it out. Check the receipt: if the service charge is a few pence more than 12.5% of the food and drink alone, it's been added to the cover charge too. On a £1.50 cover that's under 20p a head, so it's rarely worth arguing about.

How do you split a cover charge fairly?

A cover charge is per person, so the fair way is simple: each person pays their own cover. Don't fold it into the total and split by spend, because the person who had a starter shouldn't pay more cover than the person who had three courses.

Here's a worked example. Four friends eat at a restaurant with a £1.50 cover charge and a discretionary 12.5% service charge, which on this bill is added to everything.

What they had Cover Subtotal Plus 12.5% service Pays
Sam £38.00 £1.50 £39.50 £4.94 £44.44
Ella £27.00 £1.50 £28.50 £3.56 £32.06
Jo £33.00 £1.50 £34.50 £4.31 £38.81
Max £22.00 £1.50 £23.50 £2.94 £26.44
Total £120.00 £6.00 £126.00 £15.75 £141.75

Each person pays their own items plus their own £1.50 cover, then the service charge is spread in proportion to what each person had. The shares add up to the £141.75 bill exactly. Our guide to splitting service charge fairly explains why proportional is fairer than equal.

Tricky cases:

  • Someone joined for one drink. Ask the staff whether the cover applies to them. If it does, it's theirs to pay, since it's per person.
  • Children. Ask whether the cover applies to them. If it does, the child's cover goes with their parent's share.
  • Someone left early and paid cash. Make sure their cash covered their cover charge too, or it ends up spread across everyone else.

How to check the bill for a cover charge

Before you pay, look for three things:

  1. A cover line. Does the number of covers match the number of people at the table?
  2. The service charge. Is it discretionary or compulsory, and was it worked out on the cover charge as well?
  3. The menu. If you're unsure whether the cover charge was mentioned, ask to see the menu again. It's usually in small print at the bottom.

If you're paying for the table and splitting it afterwards, it's worth taking a photo of the receipt before you leave so everyone can see the cover line.

Questions people ask

Is a cover charge legal in the UK?

Yes, as long as it's clearly shown before you order, normally on the menu. The CMA's price transparency guidance lists mandatory restaurant cover charges as mandatory charges, which must be given up front.

Can I refuse to pay a cover charge?

If it wasn't shown before you ordered, you can ask for it to be removed. If it was on the menu, it's generally part of the price, though some restaurants will still take it off if you ask politely.

What's the difference between a cover charge and a service charge?

A cover charge is a flat fee per person, usually part of the price. A service charge is a percentage of the bill, usually optional, and since October 2024 it must go to staff.

Does the cover charge go to the waiter?

Not necessarily. Businesses usually keep cover charges as part of the price. Whether they should be shared with staff under the 2023 tips law is being tested in a tribunal claim against Harrods.

How do you split a cover charge in a group?

Each person pays their own cover, since it's charged per head. Then spread any service charge in proportion to what each person had.
