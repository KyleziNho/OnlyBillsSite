Leaving drinks: who pays?

Who pays at leaving drinks: the person leaving, the boss or everyone? How to handle rounds, tabs, the boss's card and a leaving meal, with £ examples.

At leaving drinks, the usual answer is that everyone pays for their own drinks, the person leaving doesn't pay for anyone else, and it's a nice gesture (not an obligation) for a manager or the team to buy the leaver's drinks. If the boss puts a card behind the bar, that covers what it covers, and after that it's back to everyone paying their own way.

Most of the awkwardness comes from rounds with too many people in them, tabs nobody's watching, and a leaving meal where no one decided in advance who covers the leaver. Here's how to handle each one.

Who pays for the person leaving?

Nobody has to, but it's common and kind for the team to cover the leaver's drinks or meal. They're the guest of honour. If you're organising, decide it in the invite so nobody's surprised:

Drinks for Jo's last day, Friday from 5.30 at The Crown 🍻 We'll get Jo's drinks between us, so if you're coming, chuck £5 in when you can: [link]. Everyone else, it's pay your own way.

A small collection like this is simpler than trying to remember who bought Jo which pint.

The leaver doesn't need to buy a round for everyone either, especially at the end of a job. If they want to, it's their call.

Rounds: when they work and when they don't

Rounds work with three or four people drinking at the same pace. They stop working with a group of twelve, where a round can cost £70 and half the table leaves before it's their turn.

Better options for a big leaving do:

  • Mini rounds: buy for the two or three people you're standing with.
  • Pay as you go: everyone gets their own. Nobody minds at a work do.
  • Opt out out loud: "I'm not in the round, I'm driving" is all you need to say.

If you've been in a round and have to go before your turn, offer to settle up: "I owe you one, can I send you a fiver?"

The boss's card behind the bar

This is the classic leaving drinks setup, and it's generous. To stop it going wrong:

  1. Ask the bar to set a limit. Most pubs will run a tab to a fixed amount and tell you when it's reached.
  2. Tell people what's on it. "Drinks are on Sam up to £150, then it's your own." Some managers limit it to beer, wine and soft drinks.
  3. Have someone watch it. The bad bills come from the gap between the tab running out and anyone noticing.
  4. Close it at a set time. Say 7pm. Then people know when the free drinks end.

If you're the manager and you're not sure whether your employer will pay you back, check the expenses policy first. A card behind the bar that you end up covering personally is a pricier goodbye than you planned.

Running a group tab

Sometimes a group opens a tab on one card and settles up at the end. It's quick at the bar but messy afterwards, because the receipt lists every drink with no names. If you do it:

  • Keep it to people who'll be there until the end.
  • Ask for an itemised receipt when you close it.
  • Split it by who had what, or evenly if everyone drank about the same. The fairness rules are the same as for dinner: see how to split a bill fairly.

The leaving meal

A sit-down meal before or instead of drinks is easier to keep fair. Agree two things before booking:

  1. Are we covering the leaver? Usually yes.
  2. Are we splitting evenly or by what people had? With colleagues you don't all know well, splitting by item avoids people quietly subsidising the wine.

Worked example

Nine colleagues take Jo out for a leaving lunch. The bill, including 12.5% service, is £297. Each person's share by what they ordered (service included) is worked out first. Jo's share is £32.

The eight others split Jo's £32 between them: £4 each, added to their own share. So someone whose own lunch came to £28.50 pays £32.50, and Jo pays nothing.

Splitting the whole £297 evenly between the eight payers would be £37.13 each, which overcharges anyone who had a sandwich and a lime soda. The same approach works for birthdays: see birthday dinner: who pays?

The leaving collection and gift

The card and gift are separate from the drinks. Keep them separate.

  • One person runs the collection, ideally someone who isn't the leaver's manager, so nobody feels watched.
  • Suggest an amount, or say "anything you like". Some people give £2, some £20.
  • A payment link or a bank transfer is easier than an envelope now, since few people carry cash. Say who to pay and by when.

When a colleague owes you money

It happens more than people admit. In a 2023 Starling Bank survey of 2,000 UK adults, a third said they were currently owed money by friends, family, partners or colleagues, and nearly half (46%) admitted feeling too awkward to ask for it. With colleagues it's easier on Monday than never:

Hey, from Friday: your share of the leaving lunch was £36.25. Here are my details whenever you get a sec 🙂

Questions people ask

Does the person leaving pay for drinks?

No, not usually. They pay for their own unless the team is covering them, which is common. They don't need to buy a round for everyone.

Should the boss pay for leaving drinks?

There's no rule, but many managers put a card behind the bar or buy the first round. Some employers pay for it as a business expense. It's a gesture, not an obligation.

How much should you put in a leaving collection?

Whatever you're comfortable with. A few pounds is normal for someone you didn't work closely with, more for a close colleague. Collections are usually voluntary and anonymous.

Should you split a leaving meal evenly?

Only if everyone ordered about the same. With colleagues it's usually fairer to pay for what you had and share the leaver's portion between everyone else.
