Splitting the bill equally could be costing you hundreds

UK diners who lose out on an equal split estimate about £8.73 a meal. Here's what that adds up to over a year, with a worked example and a quick fix.

If you usually order less than your friends, splitting the bill equally quietly costs you money every time. People in the UK who feel they lose out estimate it at about £8.73 a meal (OpenTable UK survey, 2016), which is easily £100 to £400 a year if you eat out regularly.

It doesn't feel like much on the night. That's the point. Nobody wants to argue over £8, so nobody does, and the same people end up topping up the same people for years.

What the research actually says

In May 2016 OpenTable surveyed UK diners about splitting the bill. The headline findings:

  • 2 in 3 diners said they'll split the bill when eating out, despite worrying about losing out.
  • 38% said they feel they lose money when splitting, an estimated £8.73 per meal.
  • 58% said they agree to split equally simply because it makes paying easier.
  • 34% said they split so others don't think they're tight.
  • 54% said they avoid splitting when others drink a lot.

OpenTable then scaled the £8.73 up to a potential £38,782 over a lifetime. Treat that number with care. It's people's own estimate multiplied out, OpenTable didn't publish the sample size, and the survey is now ten years old. For a sense of scale, £38,782 divided by £8.73 is about 4,440 meals. That's roughly six group meals a month for 60 years, which is a lot of dinners.

So the lifetime figure is a headline. The per-meal figure is the useful part, because you can check it against your own habits.

A worked example: one dinner

Four friends go for dinner. Here's what each of them actually ordered:

Person What they had Cost
Sam Pasta and a lemonade £17.00
Aisha Burger and a pint £22.00
Ben Steak frites and a soft drink £26.00
Chloe Starter, steak, two glasses of wine £35.00
Total £100.00

The restaurant adds a 12.5% service charge, so the bill comes to £112.50.

Split equally, that's £28.125 each, so in practice two people pay £28.13 and two pay £28.12.

Split by what each person had, with the service charge shared in proportion:

Person Fair share Pays if split equally Difference
Sam £19.13 £28.13 pays £9.00 more
Aisha £24.75 £28.13 pays £3.38 more
Ben £29.25 £28.12 saves £1.13
Chloe £39.37 £28.12 saves £11.25

(Chloe's exact share is £39.375 and Sam's is £19.125. One rounds up and one rounds down so the shares still add up to £112.50.)

Nobody did anything wrong here. Chloe didn't plan to be subsidised. But Sam paid £9 towards other people's food and drink, and Chloe saved £11.25. An equal split doesn't make the cost disappear, it just moves it from the people who ordered more to the people who ordered less.

Why the gap tends to grow

There's a second cost that's easy to miss. When a table splits equally, people tend to order more.

In a restaurant experiment by Gneezy, Haruvy and Yafe (2004), diners who knew the bill would be split equally spent about 36% more on average than diners paying for themselves. The reason is simple: if six of you split, an extra £6 dessert only costs you £1. Everyone thinks the same way, so the whole bill goes up. We've written up the full experiment and what it means.

The study was small (groups of six students in Israel), so don't read the exact percentage as gospel. But it means an equal split can cost the light orderer twice: once for the gap, and again for the bigger bill.

Who loses out most

You're likely overpaying on an equal split if you:

  • Don't drink, or drink less than the table (drinks are often the biggest gap; see splitting when some people don't drink).
  • Usually skip starters or dessert.
  • Go out with a mixed group where incomes differ a lot.
  • Are the person who always says "it's fine, let's just split it".

The people who come out ahead are usually not being cheeky. Often they simply never see the numbers.

When an equal split is still fine

Equal splitting isn't always wrong. It works well when:

  • Everyone ordered roughly the same (within a few pounds of each other).
  • You shared everything, like tapas or a curry for the table.
  • It's a set menu or a fixed price per head.
  • Your group takes turns and it genuinely evens out.

A good rule of thumb: if anyone would pay more than about £5 over what they had, split by item instead. There's more on that in is splitting the bill equally fair?

How to stop overpaying without being awkward

You don't need to audit the receipt in front of everyone. A few things that work:

Say it before you order, not after. It's much easier to agree a method at the start. Try:

"Shall we just pay for what we each have tonight? I'm only having a main."

Offer the middle ground. Split the food equally and pay for your own drinks. That takes care of most of the gap with almost no maths.

Be the one who sorts it. Whoever pays at the till can work out each share and send everyone a total. People rarely object to a fair number they didn't have to calculate.

"I've paid. You're each £24.75, £29.25 and £39.37. Pay me back whenever."

Questions people ask

How much do you lose by splitting the bill equally?

It depends on how differently you order. In OpenTable's 2016 UK survey, people who felt they lost out estimated about £8.73 per meal. At two group meals a month, that's about £210 a year.

Is splitting the bill equally ever cheaper?

Yes, for whoever ordered the most. Over a year it usually evens out only if everyone orders about the same, or if your group genuinely takes turns being the big spender.

Do people order more when the bill is split?

Research suggests they do. In Gneezy, Haruvy and Yafe's 2004 experiment, diners spent about 36% more when they knew the bill would be split equally. It was a small study, so treat the exact figure with caution.

How do I suggest paying separately without seeming tight?

Bring it up before anyone orders, keep it about you rather than them ("I'm only having a main"), and offer to work out the numbers. Most people are relieved someone else suggested it.
