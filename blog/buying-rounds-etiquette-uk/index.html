Buying rounds in a UK pub: the etiquette

How rounds work in a UK pub, the unwritten rules, what to do when someone skips their round or drinks pricier drinks, and when a tab or split is fairer.

A round is when one person buys drinks for everyone in the group, then the next person buys the next lot, until everyone has had a turn. The main rules are simple: get your round in without being reminded, keep your order similar to what you'd normally drink, and don't leave before it's your turn. Rounds work best with three to five people drinking at a similar pace. With bigger groups or very different drinks, a tab or paying for your own is fairer.

Rounds are a proper British habit, but they're less universal than they used to be.

In a June 2025 YouGov poll of 6,587 adults in Great Britain (reported by the Morning Advertiser), 48% said they'd rather buy drinks just for themselves, 38% preferred taking turns, and 14% didn't know. Age makes a big difference. Among 18 to 24 year olds, 61% preferred not to do rounds. People aged 65 and over were the most likely to prefer them, at 46%. So if your group can't agree, you're not alone.

How do rounds work in a pub?

The person who buys the first round goes to the bar and gets a drink for everyone in the group. When glasses are nearly empty, the next person goes. You carry on until everyone has bought one round, then either start again or call it a night.

A few things everyone is expected to know:

  • You're in or you're out. Once you've accepted a drink in a round, you're in, and you owe a round back.
  • Order what you'd normally drink. The person buying shouldn't have to wonder if you'll suddenly want a double.
  • Don't wait to be asked. Debrett's puts it plainly: "you should never have to be reminded it is your turn."
  • Go early if you're leaving early. If you know you have to go at 9pm, buy the first or second round, not the last one you'll never reach.
  • Help carry. If someone is buying for six, go with them.

What's the etiquette if you only want one drink?

Say so before the first round, not after you've been bought a drink. Something like:

"I'm only staying for one, so I'll just get my own. Don't count me in the round."

That's completely normal and nobody minds. The awkward version is accepting three drinks across three rounds and then leaving before your turn.

The same goes if you're driving, not drinking, or on a budget. Opting out out loud is far better than drifting in and out.

What if someone drinks pricier drinks than everyone else?

This is where rounds quietly stop being fair. Debrett's advice is not to "opt for overly expensive orders (for example a beer and a chaser) when other people are buying a round."

Here's what the gap looks like. Four friends do four rounds. Three drink pints at £6.20. Sam drinks gin and tonics at £9.50.

Each round costs What each person drinks across 4 rounds What each person pays
Pint drinkers (×3) £28.10 £24.80 £28.10
Sam £28.10 £38.00 £28.10

Each round costs £28.10 (three pints plus one G&T), whoever buys it. So everyone pays the same, £28.10. But each pint drinker only drank £24.80, so they each pay £3.30 for Sam's gin. Sam drinks £38 and pays £28.10, which is £9.90 of drinks paid for by everyone else.

£3.30 each isn't a fortune over one evening. But if the gap is bigger (cocktails versus halves) or it happens every week, it's worth sorting. Some fixes:

  • The pricier drinker offers to top up. "My drinks are dearer, let me get the crisps and a round of shots" or just sending a few quid.
  • The pricier drinker buys their own. They stay in the round for company but get their own G&Ts.
  • Switch to a tab and split it by what people had (more below).

If you're the one on the pricier drinks, the kind thing is to bring it up first, so no one else has to.

What do you do when someone skips their round?

Usually it's not deliberate. People lose count, need to leave or run out of money. Start by assuming the best.

If they're still there, a light nudge does the job:

"Is it your round or mine? I've lost track."

If they left before their turn, the fair fix is for them to pay back what they drank, not the cost of a whole round. Say five friends are buying £6 pints and Alex leaves after three rounds without buying one. Alex drank three £6 pints, bought by three different people. The simple fix is £6 to each of those three. That's fairer than asking for £30, because Alex wasn't there to drink the rest.

A message for the person who left:

"Hey, no worries about last night. You had a pint in my round, Jo's and Priya's. Just send us £6 each when you get a sec."

If you're the one who left early:

"Sorry I had to dash before my round. I had three pints, so I'll send £6 each to you, Jo and Priya."

If it keeps happening with the same person, it's fine to change the system rather than have the same awkward chat every week. "Shall we just get our own tonight?" works without blaming anyone. Our guide on what to do when someone won't pay their share covers the harder conversations.

What if the night ends before everyone has bought a round?

This is the most common problem with rounds and it's nobody's fault. Five friends, but last orders comes after four rounds. One person has bought nothing.

You can let it go, especially if you meet up often and it will even out. Or the person who missed out buys the first round next time. Saying it out loud helps:

"I'll get the first one next time, I owe you."

If it won't even out (a one off night, or a work do), settle up in money instead. Work out what one round cost and have the person who didn't buy send their share to the people who did.

When should you switch to a tab or split instead?

Rounds stop working once the group or the drinks get too uneven. Switch when:

  • The group is more than five or six. A round for ten can easily cost £60 or more, and half the group may leave before their turn. At leaving drinks, mini rounds with the two or three people you're standing with work better.
  • Drinks vary a lot. Pints, cocktails, soft drinks and wine in the same group.
  • People are drinking at different speeds. One person on their fourth while another is still on their first.
  • Someone isn't drinking. They shouldn't be in a round at all. See how to split the bill when some people don't drink.
  • You're on a budget. On student nights out, buying your own often keeps everyone happier.

Your own drinks. Everyone goes to the bar for themselves. The fairest and the simplest, though it means more trips.

A kitty. Everyone puts in the same amount (say £20) and one person buys from it. Good for groups drinking similar drinks. Less good if one person drinks much more than another.

A tab, split at the end. One person opens a tab with their card, everyone orders on it, and you split the itemised receipt at the end. Ask the bar for an itemised bill rather than just the total, so you can see each drink.

Here's a tab for five people that ended at £153.40:

Drinks Drinks total
Jo 4 pints at £6.20 £24.80
Priya 3 large wines at £9.80 £29.40
Alex 2 pints, then 2 cocktails at £12 £36.40
Sam 4 G&Ts at £9.50 £38.00
Ellie 3 lime and sodas at £3.50, one pint £16.70

Add Jo's crisps (£2.10) and a bowl of chips for the table (£6.00, split four ways because Ellie didn't have any) and Jo pays £28.40, Priya £30.90, Alex £37.90, Sam £39.50 and Ellie £16.70. Total: £153.40. If you'd split it equally at £30.68 each, Ellie would have paid £13.98 more than she had.

Questions people ask

Is it rude not to buy a round?

Only if you've accepted drinks in one and then don't buy back. Saying "I'll just get my own tonight" before the first round is completely normal, and around half of adults in Great Britain say they prefer buying for themselves (YouGov, 2025).

Do you have to buy a round if you only had one drink?

No. If you've only had one drink in the round, offer to buy the next one or send the person who bought it the price of your drink. Then step out of the round if you're not staying.

How many people should be in a round?

Three to five works best. With more than that, rounds get expensive and people often leave before their turn. Split into smaller rounds or switch to a tab.

What do you say if someone never buys their round?

Start light: "Is it your round or mine?" If it keeps happening, suggest everyone gets their own for a while. It changes the system without accusing anyone.
