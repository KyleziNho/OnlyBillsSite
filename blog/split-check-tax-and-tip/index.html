How to split a check with tax and tip

Split tax and tip in proportion to what each person ordered. The one-line formula, a worked $183.78 example, tip before or after tax, and the last cent.

To split a check fairly with tax and tip, each person pays for their own items, then adds the same percentage of tax and tip on top. In one line: your share = your items ÷ subtotal × total. So if you ordered a quarter of the food, you pay a quarter of the tax and a quarter of the tip.

Below: a worked example, the questions that come up every time (before or after tax? what about shared appetizers? who gets the extra cent?) and what the averages say about how much to tip.

The fair formula

Three numbers from the check:

  • Subtotal: the food and drinks, before tax.
  • Tax and tip: everything added on top.
  • Total: what the card was charged.

Divide the total by the subtotal to get a multiplier. Multiply each person's items by it. Everyone pays the same tax rate (which is how sales tax works anyway) and tips at the same rate.

A worked example: four friends, one check

Four friends eat out in New York City, where the combined sales tax is 8.875%. The subtotal is $142.60. They tip 20% on the pre-tax amount.

  • Tax: 8.875% of $142.60 = $12.66
  • Tip: 20% of $142.60 = $28.52
  • Total: $183.78

Each person's share of tax and tip follows what they ordered:

Person Ordered Tax Tip Fair share Even split
Ana $22.40 $1.99 $4.48 $28.87 $45.95
Ben $48.75 $4.33 $9.75 $62.83 $45.95
Cal $31.20 $2.77 $6.24 $40.21 $45.94
Dee $40.25 $3.57 $8.05 $51.87 $45.94
Total $142.60 $12.66 $28.52 $183.78 $183.78

On an even split, Ana pays $17.08 more than her own dinner cost, and Ben pays $16.88 less. That's the whole argument for itemizing: the person who ordered a salad isn't paying for the steak, or tipping on it.

The multiplier version gives the same answer: $183.78 ÷ $142.60 = 1.2888, and Ana's $22.40 × 1.2888 = $28.87.

Should you tip before or after tax?

Most people tip on the pre-tax subtotal, and that's the usual etiquette advice. Tax isn't part of the service. Tipping on the after-tax total is a little more generous and perfectly fine if your group prefers it.

The difference is small. In the example above, 20% of the pre-tax $142.60 is $28.52. Twenty percent of the after-tax $155.26 is $31.05, so $2.53 more across the table.

Whichever you pick, the split works the same way: each person's share of the tip follows their share of the food.

How much to tip in 2026

There's no law setting a tip in the US, and tipping is voluntary unless a gratuity is printed on the check. What people actually do, according to card and digital payments on Toast's restaurant platform in Q2 2026:

Type of restaurant Average tip, Q2 2026
Full-service (sit-down) 19.1%
Quick-service (counter) 15.8%

Toast's figures leave out cash tips and only cover its own restaurants, so treat them as a guide, not a rule. For a sit-down meal with good service, 18% to 20% is normal. Tip prompts on the payment screen often start higher. You're free to pick a custom amount.

Shared appetizers and pitchers

Split a shared dish only between the people who had it. If Ana, Cal and Dee share $14.00 of nachos and Ben doesn't touch them, the nachos go $4.67, $4.67 and $4.66 to those three. Then their tax and tip follow, because the nachos are now part of their items.

The same goes for a pitcher of beer or a bottle of wine. The non-drinker shouldn't be tipping on it. If you're doing this by hand, add the shared dishes to each sharer's items first, then apply the multiplier.

Automatic gratuity for large parties

Many restaurants add a gratuity, often 18% to 20%, for parties of six or more. It's printed on the check as "gratuity", "auto-grat" or "service charge". Check before anyone adds a tip on the card slip, or the group tips twice.

Split an automatic gratuity exactly like a tip: in proportion. If the service was great and you want to add a little more, add it to the total and let the multiplier spread it. There's more on extras in how to split service charge and tips fairly.

Rounding so the shares add up to the check

Percentages rarely land on whole cents, so rounded shares can come out a cent over or under the total. Three people splitting $100.00 evenly owe $33.333 each. Round all three to $33.33 and you're a cent short.

The clean fix:

  1. Round everyone's share down to the cent.
  2. Add up the rounded shares and see how many cents are missing.
  3. Give one extra cent to the people whose shares were rounded down the most, one cent each, until it adds up.

For $100.00 three ways: $33.34, $33.33, $33.33. It sounds fussy, but it's the difference between a split that matches the card charge and one where the payer quietly loses a few cents every time.

Questions people ask

Do you tip on the tax?

You don't have to. The usual convention is to tip on the pre-tax subtotal. Tipping on the total after tax is slightly more generous: at 20% with 8.875% tax, about $1.78 more per $100 of food. Either is fine; just agree on one before you split.

Is 20% the standard tip in 2026?

At sit-down restaurants, 20% is the common rule of thumb for good service. Toast's card data puts the actual average at 19.1% for full-service restaurants and 15.8% for quick-service in Q2 2026.

How do you split a tip unevenly?

In proportion to what each person ordered. Work out each person's share of the subtotal, then give them the same share of the tip. Someone who ordered 30% of the food pays 30% of the tip.

Should the person who ordered more pay more tip?

Yes. A tip is a percentage of the bill, so each person's tip should be the same percentage of what they ordered. Splitting the tip equally makes light eaters tip at a higher rate than everyone else.

What if the restaurant already added an 18% gratuity?

Then the tip is already on the check, so don't add another 20% on top unless you want to. Split the automatic gratuity in proportion like any tip. Read the check: it's usually printed as "gratuity" or "service charge" for parties of six or more.
