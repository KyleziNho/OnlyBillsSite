Is splitting the bill equally fair?

Splitting equally is fair when everyone had about the same. When orders differ by more than £5, it isn't. Here's a simple rule, worked examples and what to say.

Splitting the bill equally is fair when everyone ordered roughly the same amount or genuinely shared the food. It stops being fair when one person's order is a lot cheaper than the average, because they end up paying for other people's meals.

A simple rule of thumb: if anyone would pay more than about £5 over what they actually had, don't split equally. Below that, the time and awkwardness of itemising usually isn't worth it.

of UK diners feel they lose out on money when the bill is split

Why so many people split equally anyway

Splitting equally is popular because it's easy, not because people think it's fair. In a 2016 OpenTable survey of UK diners, 58% said they agree to split equally because it makes paying easier, and 34% said they do it so others don't think they're tight. If you want the money side of that in numbers, we've worked out what an equal split costs you over a year.

A 2021 comparethemarket.com survey of 2,000 diners found the country almost exactly divided: 34% think you should split straight down the middle, while 36% think each person should pay for what they had. The same survey found 42% had paid more than they wanted to avoid an awkward moment, so if you've ever quietly done that, you're in good company.

The £5 rule

Work out the equal share (the bill divided by the number of people). Then look at the cheapest order at the table.

  • If the cheapest order is within about £5 of the equal share: split equally. The difference is small and it's quicker.
  • If it's more than £5 below: pay by item, or at least let that person pay for what they had.

Why £5? It's roughly the point where people start to mind, and roughly the cost of a drink. You can move the line for your group. For a cheap lunch, £3 might be the line. For a £100 a head dinner, 10% of the equal share is a better guide.

Example 1: equal is fine

Four friends get pizza:

Person Ordered
Jo £21
Dan £23
Mei £24
Ravi £24
Total £92

The equal share is £23. The cheapest order (Jo, £21) is only £2 below it. Split equally. Nobody will notice £2, and it saves five minutes at the table.

Example 2: equal isn't fair

Four colleagues go for dinner:

Person Ordered Equal share Difference
Hannah (main, tap water) £15 £28 pays £13 more
Kofi (main, one beer) £22 £28 pays £6 more
Lucy (two courses, wine) £30 £28 saves £2
Tom (three courses, cocktails) £45 £28 saves £17
Total £112

Hannah's order is £13 below the equal share, well over the £5 line. On an equal split, Hannah and Kofi together put £19 towards Lucy and Tom's food and drink. Pay by item.

Example 3: the middle ground

Sometimes itemising everything feels like overkill, but splitting equally clearly isn't right. Try splitting the food equally and paying for drinks separately.

Take a table of three sharing a curry:

  • Shared food (curries, rice, naan for the table): £54
  • Drinks: Amir two pints (£12), Beth a bottle of wine she mostly drank herself (£24), Cal a lime soda (£3)
  • Total: £93
Person Food share Own drinks Pays
Amir £18 £12 £30
Beth £18 £24 £42
Cal £18 £3 £21
Total £54 £39 £93

An equal split would have been £31 each. Cal would have paid £10 more than his share and Beth £11 less. Splitting the food and keeping drinks separate fixes almost all of that with two lines of maths. Drinks are usually where the gap comes from; there's more on that in splitting the bill when some people don't drink.

When splitting equally is the fair choice

Equal really is the fairest option when:

  • You shared everything. Tapas, a Chinese banquet, a curry for the table. Trying to itemise who ate how much prawn toast is silly.
  • It's a set menu or a fixed price per head.
  • Everyone ordered about the same (the £5 rule).
  • Your group takes turns being the big spender and you all know it evens out.
  • It's a celebration and you've agreed it up front, like everyone chipping in for the birthday person (see who pays at a birthday dinner).

When it usually isn't fair

Equal is often unfair when:

  • Someone doesn't drink and others are on wine or cocktails.
  • Someone is on a tighter budget and ordered carefully to stay within it.
  • The group is big. At a table of ten, small differences stack up, and people tend to order more when they know the bill is split (see why groups order more when splitting).
  • One person arrived late and only had a drink.
  • One person ordered the most expensive thing on the menu, or a second bottle nobody else wanted.
  • Couples and singles are mixed, and the couples are sharing bottles (see how to split the bill when there are couples).

How to bring it up without sounding tight

The best time is before anyone orders. Afterwards, it can feel like you're accusing someone. If you're the one who ordered less, here's how to say you only want to pay for what you had, with lines for the group chat and the table. Some lines you can copy:

Before ordering:

"Shall we just pay for our own tonight? I'm keeping it cheap."

When someone suggests splitting and you had much less:

"Would you mind if I just pay for mine? I only had the pasta and a water, so it's about £15."

If you're the one who ordered more:

"I had loads more than you, I'll pay for mine. Don't split it."

That last one is worth saying out loud more often. People who ordered less rarely want to raise it, so the person who ordered more offering first takes the awkwardness away.

In a group chat afterwards:

"I paid the bill. I've worked out what everyone had: Hannah £15, Kofi £22, Lucy £30, Tom £45. Pay me back when you can."

A fair split, step by step

If you're doing it by hand:

  1. Add up what each person ordered on their own.
  2. Add up the shared items and divide them between the people who shared them.
  3. Spread the service charge, tip or any discount in proportion to each person's total, not equally (see how to split service charge fairly).
  4. Check that the shares add up to the bill. If you're a penny out, someone takes the odd penny.

Questions people ask

Is it rude to not split the bill equally?

No. Asking to pay for what you had is normal, especially if you raise it before ordering and keep it about your own order. Most people would rather you said something than quietly resented it.

When should you split the bill equally?

When everyone ordered about the same (within about £5 of each other), when you shared all the food, or for a set menu. Otherwise paying by item is fairer.

What percentage of people prefer to split the bill equally?

Opinion is split almost down the middle. In a 2021 comparethemarket.com survey of 2,000 diners, 34% said split down the middle and 36% said pay for what you had.

How do you split a bill when one person had a lot more?

Let everyone pay for what they had, or split the shared food equally and let people pay for their own drinks and extras. The person who ordered more offering to pay their share first helps a lot.
