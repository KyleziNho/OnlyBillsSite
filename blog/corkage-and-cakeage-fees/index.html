Corkage and cakeage fees: costs and how to split them

Corkage is a per bottle fee for bringing your own wine, cakeage a per head fee for your own cake. Typical UK costs, and how to split them when only some had it.

Corkage is a fee a restaurant charges to open and serve a bottle of wine you brought yourself, usually per bottle. Cakeage is the same idea for a birthday cake you bring in, usually charged per person. In the UK, corkage on a still wine is typically somewhere around £12 to £15 a bottle, and reported cakeage fees run from a few pounds to about £10 a head. When it's time to split, the fee goes with the thing it's for: only the people who drank the wine share the corkage, and only the people who had cake share the cakeage.

What is corkage?

Corkage is what a restaurant charges when you bring your own bottle (BYO, or BYOB). It's meant to cover serving the wine, the glasses and the wine sales the restaurant misses out on.

It's normally charged per bottle opened, and it appears on the receipt as a line like "Corkage × 2". Some restaurants charge only a few pounds. Others don't allow it at all.

How much is corkage in the UK?

It varies a lot, and the more expensive the restaurant, the higher it tends to be. Square, the card payments company, gave these approximate UK figures in September 2025:

Bottle Typical corkage
Still wine about £12 to £15
Regular sparkling (Cava, Prosecco) about £15 to £20
Champagne about £20 to £35

Square also noted that some venues charge as little as £3 to £5 a bottle and others £45 to £65.

At the top end it can be much more. Restaurant Online reported in June 2024 that Dorian in Notting Hill had raised its corkage from £25 to £100 a bottle, and asked guests to order a bottle of similar value from its own list too. Hélène Darroze at The Connaught also charged £100. At the other end, Sam's Riverside in Brentford charged £20 for still wine and £40 for sparkling, and waived corkage on Tuesday nights. Alain Ducasse at The Dorchester didn't allow BYO at all.

Prices and policies change, so always check when you book.

Is bringing your own wine worth it?

Only if your bottle plus corkage costs less than something similar on the wine list. A quick check:

  • Your bottle cost £12 in the shop. Corkage is £15. That's £27 in total.
  • A similar bottle on the list is £36.

You save £9. But if corkage were £25, you'd pay £37 and be worse off. BYO makes most sense for a special bottle you already own, or at restaurants with low or no corkage.

What is cakeage?

Cakeage is a fee for bringing your own cake, usually for a birthday. Restaurants say it covers staff time, table space and the desserts you're not ordering. It's usually per person, not per cake.

How much is cakeage in the UK?

There's no standard, and many places don't charge at all. Some reported examples:

  • £10 a head at an unnamed London restaurant, which screenwriter Ivor Baddiel tweeted about in April 2022 (Time Out, The Caterer).
  • £7 a head at Sushisamba's London branches, and £12.50 a head at another unnamed London restaurant, according to The Caterer (April 2022).
  • About £9 a head at the Blind Bull pub in Buxton, roughly the price of a dessert (Morning Advertiser, 2023).

Plenty of pubs and restaurants don't charge. In the same Caterer piece, operators were split: some said a fee was fair because a lost dessert sale is lost margin, others disagreed, one saying "throwing a few quid for a bit of cake isn't hospitality". Pubs quoted by the Morning Advertiser said they let people bring cakes for free but like to be asked first.

These figures are from 2022 and 2023 reports, so treat them as examples rather than today's going rate.

How do you split corkage when only some people drank the wine?

Treat the corkage as part of the bottle. Whoever shares the bottle shares its corkage.

Worked example. Four friends go to a BYO restaurant. Ana brings two bottles she bought for £12 each, and they've agreed to split the cost of the wine between whoever drinks it. Corkage is £15 a bottle.

  • Bottle 1 is shared by Ana, Ben and Cal.
  • Bottle 2 is shared by Ana and Ben.
  • Dee doesn't drink.

Each bottle really costs £27 (£12 from the shop plus £15 corkage).

Bottle 1 (£27 ÷ 3) Bottle 2 (£27 ÷ 2) Wine and corkage total
Ana £9.00 £13.50 £22.50
Ben £9.00 £13.50 £22.50
Cal £9.00 £9.00
Dee £0.00
Total £27.00 £27.00 £54.00

In practice it's in two parts, because the £30 corkage is on the restaurant bill and the £24 for the wine was paid by Ana in the shop:

  • On the restaurant bill: corkage shares are Ana £12.50, Ben £12.50, Cal £5.00 and Dee nothing. That adds up to £30.
  • Back to Ana for the wine: Ben sends her £10 (£4 for bottle 1 plus £6 for bottle 2) and Cal sends £4.

Everyone's food is split separately, however you normally split it. Dee, who didn't drink, pays nothing towards the wine or the corkage.

If someone brings a bottle as a gift for the table, the wine is their treat, but it's kind to offer to cover the corkage between the people who drink it. A £15 corkage shared by three is £5 each.

If the restaurant adds a service charge, it's often worked out on the whole bill, corkage included. Spread it in proportion to what each person had, so the drinkers pay the service on the corkage. Our guide to splitting service charge fairly shows how.

How do you split cakeage at a birthday dinner?

The birthday person shouldn't pay for their own cake, so they shouldn't pay their own cakeage either. Split it, and the cake itself, between everyone else who had some.

Worked example. Six friends go out for Mia's birthday. Leo bought the cake for £30. The restaurant charges £5 a head cakeage for everyone who has a slice, and all six do, so that's £30 on the bill.

  • Cake: £30 ÷ 5 = £6 each for the five guests (sent to Leo, who bought it).
  • Cakeage: £30 ÷ 5 = £6 each for the five guests (on the restaurant bill).
  • Mia pays nothing for either.

So each guest pays £12 towards the cake and cakeage, plus their own food and drink. If someone didn't have any cake and the restaurant didn't charge for them, leave them out of both. More on covering the birthday person in birthday dinner: who pays?

What to say when you book

Most corkage and cakeage awkwardness comes from finding out at the end. A quick message when you book sorts it.

Asking about corkage:

"Hi, we've got a table for four on Saturday. Do you allow BYO wine, and if so, how much is corkage per bottle?"

Asking about cakeage:

"We're celebrating a birthday. Would it be OK to bring a cake, and is there a charge for that?"

In the group chat beforehand:

"The restaurant charges £15 corkage a bottle. Plan is whoever drinks the wine splits the bottle and the corkage. Food we'll split as normal."

If the fee is a surprise on the bill:

"We weren't told about a cakeage charge when we booked. Could you take it off, please?"

Being told in advance is what makes a fee feel fair. The same goes for a cover charge. If it was never mentioned, it's reasonable to ask politely for it to be removed.

Questions people ask

What does corkage mean?

Corkage is a fee a restaurant charges to open and serve wine you brought with you. It's usually charged per bottle.

How much is corkage in the UK?

Typically around £12 to £15 a bottle for still wine, more for sparkling and champagne, according to Square (2025). Some places charge a few pounds and high end restaurants can charge £100 or more.

Is cakeage normal in the UK?

Some restaurants charge it and many don't, especially pubs. Reported fees range from a few pounds to around £10 a head. Ask when you book.

Who pays the corkage when splitting the bill?

The people who drank the wine. Add the corkage to the price of the bottle and divide it between whoever shared it.

Should the birthday person pay cakeage?

No. Split the cake and any cakeage between the other guests who had some, the same way you'd cover the birthday person's meal.
