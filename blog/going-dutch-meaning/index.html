What does going Dutch mean?

Going Dutch means each person pays for themselves. Where the phrase really comes from (America, not the 17th century), how Brits use it and related terms.

Kyle, founder of OnlyBills19 October 2026 · 4 min read

Going Dutch means each person pays their own way, usually for a meal, drinks or a date, instead of one person paying for everyone. Strictly, it means everyone pays for what they had. In everyday British use it often just means splitting the bill, including splitting it evenly.

"Shall we go Dutch?" usually means "Shall we each pay our share?"

Where does "going Dutch" come from?

The short answer: it's an American phrase from the late 1800s, and it probably has less to do with the Netherlands than people assume.

The older form is "Dutch treat", meaning an outing where everyone pays for themselves (a joke, since a "treat" usually means someone else pays). Word historians have found it in American print from the early 1870s. Dave Wilton at Wordorigins.org traces it to a University of Michigan student paper in April 1870, which already refers to "the 'Dutch treat' of former years", and Michael Quinion found it in a Missouri newspaper in June 1873. "Go Dutch" appears around the turn of the century. One of the first clear definitions is from a 1912 phrase book: "To Go Dutch is to let each man in a party pay for his own drink or refreshment" (quoted by Wordorigins.org).

Two things are uncertain:

  • Which "Dutch"? In 19th century American English, "Dutch" was often used for Germans and German speakers (from Deutsch), as in "Pennsylvania Dutch". An 1873 American newspaper column, found by the Word Histories blog, described German drinkers in the US where "each man pays for what he consumes" instead of buying rounds. So "Dutch treat" may simply have described a German custom.
  • Was it an insult? Some sources see it as a dig at supposedly stingy Dutch or German immigrants. Others read it as a plain description of a habit. There's no firm evidence either way.

The popular story that doesn't fit

You'll often read that "going Dutch" comes from 17th century rivalry between England and the Netherlands, when English picked up a number of unflattering "Dutch" phrases.

The dates don't support that. Quinion points out that the bill-splitting phrases are "too recent and from the wrong continent" for that explanation. Quinion accepts that some insulting "Dutch" phrases do date from that period, but the bill-splitting ones show up in America about two centuries later.

How people use it today

In the UK, "going Dutch" sounds a little old-fashioned but everyone knows it. It comes up most around dates: "Should we go Dutch on a first date?" is a much more common question than going Dutch with friends, where people just say "split it". (We look at what Brits think in who pays on a first date?)

There are two meanings in use, and it's worth knowing which one someone means:

What they say What they might mean
"Let's go Dutch" Each pays for exactly what they had
"Let's go Dutch" Split the total evenly

For two people with similar orders, it makes no difference. For a group where some drank and some didn't, it can mean £15 or more. If in doubt, ask: "Do you mean half each, or our own?" More on when each is fair in is splitting the bill equally fair?

Some people prefer "split the bill" simply because it's clearer.

A quick guide to going Dutch

  • Say it early. Before ordering is easiest: "Shall we go Dutch?" or "Shall we just split it?"
  • Be clear about which kind. Half each, or your own items.
  • Shared dishes and wine split between whoever had them.
  • Service charge goes in proportion to what each person spent, so the smaller order tips less.
  • Offer, don't insist. If someone wants to treat you, "That's really kind, thank you, next one's on me" is a gracious reply.

Questions people ask

What does "go Dutch" mean on a date?

That each person pays for their own food and drink, or that you split the bill between you, rather than one person paying.

Is "going Dutch" offensive?

It's not generally considered offensive, and it's widely used. Its origin may have involved a mild ethnic stereotype, but most people now use it neutrally. "Split the bill" is a clear alternative.

Why is it called going Dutch?

Nobody knows for certain. It comes from the American phrase "Dutch treat" from the 1870s, where "Dutch" may have meant German, describing immigrants who paid for themselves rather than buying rounds.

Does going Dutch mean splitting the bill 50/50?

Sometimes. Strictly, it means everyone pays for what they had, but many people use it to mean splitting evenly. It's worth checking which one is meant.
