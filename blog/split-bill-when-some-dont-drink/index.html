How to split the bill when some people don't drink

Non-drinkers often pay for everyone's wine on an equal split. Here's a fair way to split food and drinks, a worked £ example, and what to say at the table.

The fairest way to split a bill when some people don't drink is to split the food and let everyone pay for their own drinks. Shared bottles are split only between the people who drank from them. It takes about a minute, and it stops the person on lime and soda from paying for half a bottle of Rioja.

Drinks are usually the biggest gap on a restaurant bill. A bottle of wine can cost more than a main course, so an equal split moves a lot of money from non-drinkers to drinkers.

Non-drinkers aren't a small minority you can ignore. Around a quarter of adults in England didn't drink alcohol in 2024 (Health Survey for England, via Drinkaware), and plenty more are cutting down, driving home, pregnant, on medication or just not in the mood. Most groups have at least one.

A worked example

Four friends go out for dinner. Priya doesn't drink. Tom and Jess share a bottle of wine. Ali has two pints.

Priya Tom Jess Ali
Food £16.00 £18.00 £15.00 £17.00
Drinks Lime soda £3.50 Half the wine £14.00 Half the wine £14.00 Two pints £12.00
What they had £19.50 £32.00 £29.00 £29.00

The bill is £109.50.

Split equally, that's £27.375 each, so two people pay £27.38 and two pay £27.37. Priya pays about £7.88 more than she had, which goes towards everyone else's drinks.

Here are the three common ways to split it:

Method Priya Tom Jess Ali
Split equally £27.38 £27.38 £27.37 £27.37
Food equally, own drinks £20.00 £30.50 £30.50 £28.50
Everyone pays for what they had £19.50 £32.00 £29.00 £29.00

All three add up to £109.50.

The middle option is the one most groups land on. The food is split four ways (£66 ÷ 4 = £16.50 each), then each person adds their own drinks. It isn't perfect, but it fixes almost all of Priya's overpayment with very little maths. If there's a service charge, spread it in proportion to each person's total rather than equally, so the person who had less also pays less service. Our guide to splitting service charge fairly shows how.

Who should split the wine?

Only the people who drank it. If three people share a £30 bottle, it's £10 each, and the fourth person pays nothing towards it.

Some trickier cases:

  • Someone had one small glass. Either split the bottle between everyone who had any, or agree the light drinker chips in a fixed amount (the menu price of a glass is a fair guide). Keep it simple.
  • The table ordered a second bottle. Split that one only between the people who wanted it.
  • Someone orders the bottle "for the table". Ask who's having some before it arrives. "Who's in on the wine?" works fine.
  • Cocktails versus pints. Paying for your own drinks handles this automatically. A £14 cocktail and a £6 pint are very different, and nobody has to point it out.

Why non-drinkers don't say anything

Nobody wants to be the person counting the drinks. In a 2016 OpenTable survey, 34% of UK diners said they split so others don't think they're tight, and 54% said they avoid splitting when others drink a lot. That second number suggests plenty of people mind, they just don't say so at the table.

If you don't drink, you're allowed to ask. If you do drink, the kind thing is to offer before they have to.

What to say: scripts you can copy

If you don't drink, before ordering:

"I'm not drinking tonight, so shall we split the food and pay for our own drinks?"

If you don't drink, when the bill comes:

"Could I just pay for my food and my soft drink? That's £19.50."

Saying an exact number helps. It sounds organised rather than awkward.

If you're drinking and someone isn't:

"Priya wasn't drinking, so let's take the wine and beers off her share."

This is the most useful line of all. When a drinker says it, the non-drinker doesn't have to.

If you're organising a group dinner, in the group chat beforehand:

"Booked for 8pm. Plan is we split the food and everyone pays for their own drinks, so nobody's paying for anyone else's wine."

If it's a set price per head with drinks included:

"Is there a price without the drinks package? A couple of us aren't drinking."

Many restaurants will do a food only price for set menus if you ask in advance.

What if the group insists on splitting equally?

Sometimes the table just wants to split it and move on. A few options:

  • Let it go if the gap is small. If you'd only be paying £2 or £3 over, it may not be worth the conversation. (We use a £5 rule of thumb for when equal stops being fair.)
  • Pay your bit and step back. "I'll put in £20 for mine, you lot sort the rest." Most people won't argue with that.
  • Take turns. If you eat with the same group often and someone drinks much more, they could pick up the non-drinker's next coffee or round. Only works if everyone's honest about it.
  • Go somewhere drinks matter less. A café, brunch or a curry house often has a smaller gap between drinkers and non-drinkers than a wine bar.

Questions people ask

Should non-drinkers pay for alcohol when splitting the bill?

No, not if they didn't drink any. The usual fair approach is to split the food and have everyone pay for their own drinks, with shared bottles split only between the people who drank them.

How do you split a bottle of wine between some of the table?

Divide the price of the bottle by the number of people who drank from it. A £28 bottle shared by two is £14 each, and the rest of the table pays nothing towards it.

Is it rude to ask not to pay for drinks you didn't have?

No. It's a reasonable request, especially if you mention it before ordering and give a clear number. Most drinkers are happy to take the drinks off your share once it's said out loud.

What if someone only had one glass of wine?

Either include them in the bottle split or agree they pay roughly one glass's worth. Pick whatever is quickest. The main thing is that someone who had nothing pays nothing.
