Bill splitting etiquette: 12 unwritten rules for the UK

The unwritten rules of splitting a restaurant bill in the UK, from agreeing the method early to drinks, service charge and paying back the same day.

The short version of bill splitting etiquette: agree how you're paying before the bill arrives, pay for roughly what you had, cover the service charge in proportion, and pay back whoever covered it the same day. Almost every awkward moment at the end of a meal comes from one of those four going wrong.

It goes wrong a lot. In a 2021 survey of 2,000 diners for comparethemarket.com, 43% said they feel awkward about how to approach paying when they eat out, and 52% said they'd been embarrassed by how the people they ate with handled payment (comparethemarket, 2021).

1. Agree how you're splitting before you order

The fairest moment to decide is at the start, not when a £240 bill lands. "Shall we just split it?" is fine at the start, because everyone can order with that in mind. Said at the end, it can feel like an ambush to the person who had a salad and tap water. With a mix of couples and singles, agree to split per head, not per couple (splitting the bill with couples).

Why: people order differently depending on how they're paying. A well-known 2004 restaurant study found diners spent about 51 shekels each when the bill was split equally, against about 37 when each paid their own, about 36% more (Gneezy, Haruvy and Yafe, 2004). It was a small study of groups of six students in Israel, but the effect makes sense to anyone who's watched a table order a third bottle.

If you're not sure how to raise it, here's how to avoid an awkward bill split, with messages to copy for before, during and after the meal.

2. Splitting equally is fine when orders are similar

If everyone had a main, a drink and shared a few starters, splitting equally is quick and nobody loses much. Don't get out a calculator over a £2 difference.

Why: the time and goodwill it saves is worth more than the pennies. It stops being fine when one person's share is clearly more than others', usually because of drinks.

3. Drinks are where fairness lives

If some people drank and some didn't, take the drinks off before splitting. A bottle of wine shared by three people is split by those three, not by the whole table.

Why: alcohol is often the biggest difference between people's orders. In a 2016 OpenTable survey, 54% of UK diners said they avoid splitting when others drink a lot (OpenTable, 2016). There's a full guide on splitting the bill when some people don't drink.

4. Never make someone justify paying for what they had

"Do you mind if I just pay for mine?" should get the answer "of course". Nobody should have to explain their budget at a dinner table.

Why: people often go along with equal splits to avoid looking tight. In the same OpenTable survey, 34% said they split so others don't think they're tight (OpenTable, 2016). Making it easy to opt out is kind.

5. Shared plates are split by who shared them

Sides, starters and sharing platters are split between the people who ate them. If two people shared the nachos, the nachos are theirs.

Why: it's the same principle as drinks, and it stops the person who skipped starters paying for them.

6. Service charge is split in proportion, not equally

If a 12.5% service charge is on the bill, each person's part of it should match what they spent. Someone with a £20 order pays £2.50 of service. Someone with a £50 order pays £6.25.

Why: service charge is a percentage of the bill, so each person's share should be a percentage of their part of it. Splitting it equally means the lighter eaters subsidise the heavier ones. Since 1 October 2024, the Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023 means employers in England, Wales and Scotland must pass on all the tips and service charges they receive to workers, without deductions, so paying it is a fair way to reward the staff. It's still optional, though. More in our guide to splitting service charge fairly.

7. Whoever pays gets paid back the same day

If one person puts it on their card, everyone sends their share that evening. Not "next time", not "I'll get the next one" unless they've agreed to that.

Why: the person who paid is now carrying everyone's dinner on their own card, and asking for it back is the most awkward bit of the whole evening. Don't make them ask twice.

8. Round up, not down

If your share is £27.83, send £28. If you paid and everyone owes £30.04, it's fine to say "call it £30". The person who's out of pocket gets to round down, not the people paying them back.

Why: rounding in favour of the person who paid is generous. Rounding in your own favour is noticed.

9. Don't hand the waiter eight cards without asking

Splitting the bill between lots of cards at the table is normal in the UK, but ask early and keep it simple. "Could we split this four ways, please?" is easy. Eight cards for eight different amounts takes the server's time on a busy night.

Why: staff are juggling other tables. If it's a big group, one card and settle up afterwards is often kinder. See can you ask a restaurant to split the bill.

10. Late arrivals and early leavers pay for what they had

If you arrive after the starters or leave before dessert, pay your part and maybe a little more for shared things you had. Don't leave cash on the table that doesn't cover your share plus service.

Why: the people still at the table end up covering the gap, and it's rarely mentioned but always remembered.

11. If it's someone's birthday, decide in advance whether you're covering them

Many groups split the birthday person's share between everyone else. That's lovely, but it's a cost, so agree it in the group chat before the night. There's a worked example in who pays at a birthday dinner.

Why: springing it on people at the end means some feel they had no say in spending an extra £6 or £7.

12. Don't argue about pennies at the table

If the maths is a few pence out, the person who paid absorbs it or rounds it. The bill is not the place to discover someone's spreadsheet habits.

Why: the comparethemarket survey found the average group took 6 minutes 45 seconds to settle a bill and 29% had apologised for how long it was taking (comparethemarket, 2021). That's a long, awkward end to a nice night.

A quick worked example

Four friends, a £136.13 bill including 12.5% service. Two drank, two didn't.

Cara £20.00 £3.50 £23.50 £2.94 £26.44

Questions people ask

What is the etiquette for splitting the bill?

Agree how you're splitting before you order, split equally only if orders are similar, take drinks out if some didn't drink, share service charge in proportion, and pay back whoever covered it the same day.

Is it rude to ask to pay only for what you ordered?

No. It's completely reasonable, especially if you didn't drink or ordered less. Say it early and simply: "Do you mind if I just pay for mine?"

Should the person who organised the meal pay more?

No. Organising doesn't mean paying. The organiser pays their own share, like everyone else, unless they've said it's their treat.

Do you split the tip equally?

Ideally the tip or service charge is split in proportion to what each person spent, so someone with a bigger order pays a bigger part of it.
