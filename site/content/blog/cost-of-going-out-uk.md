Going out in the UK has got noticeably more expensive, and it's changing how people socialise. In an April 2026 survey for Confused.com, half of Brits said they'd turned down an invitation because it cost too much, rising to 63% of Londoners. A third said they now only go to social events for special occasions like birthdays.

The good news is that a lot of what makes a night out expensive is in your control: when you go, where you go, and how you split the bill.

- 50% | of Brits have turned down an invitation because of the cost | Confused.com, 2026

## How much does going out cost in the UK?

Confused.com's survey (OnePoll, 2,000 UK adults, April 2026) asked people what they spend on a typical outing. The averages:

| Activity | Average spend |
|---|---|
| Music event (gig, festival) | £82 |
| Sports event | £65 |
| Dinner at a restaurant | £62 |
| Night out or clubbing | £58 |
| Leisure activity (bowling, swimming) | £39 |
| Drinks in a pub or bar | £37 |
| Breakfast, brunch or lunch out | £34 |
| Cinema | £34 |
| Daytime drinks (coffee and the like) | £26 |

These are self-reported averages, so treat them as a rough guide (the London figure above comes from a smaller group within the sample, so it's indicative too). Confused.com estimated the overall cost of going out has risen about 12% on average in two years.

## How it's changing what people do

The same survey found:

- 77% say rising costs of going out have hit them over the last two years.
- 36% are going out less than they were a year ago.
- The top things people are cutting back on are eating out (41%), nights out (31%) and drinking in pubs (28%).
- People set aside about £31 a month on average for socialising, around £372 a year.

That's the backdrop for a lot of awkward group chats. When one friend suggests a £60 a head dinner, at least some of the group are quietly doing the maths.

## Why saying no isn't the only option

Turning down invitations saves money, but it costs you time with people. Instead of choosing between going and not going, you can usually change the plan so it costs less. Here's what actually makes a difference.

## 1. Go for lunch instead of dinner

In Confused.com's survey, the average spend on breakfast, brunch or lunch out was £34, against £62 for dinner. Many restaurants run cheaper lunch menus or set menus for the same kitchen. A birthday lunch on a Saturday can feel just as much of an occasion as dinner.

## 2. Look for set menus and early sittings

Set menus (two or three courses for a fixed price) make the cost predictable before anyone sits down, which helps people on a budget say yes. Some places also do cheaper early evening menus. Check the restaurant's website or ask when you book.

## 3. Say the budget out loud

The single most useful thing you can do is make the cost clear before people commit. It lets everyone decide properly instead of saying yes and stressing later.

> "Thinking of somewhere around £25 a head, does that work for everyone?"

> "I'm trying to keep it cheap this month. Could we do somewhere a bit more casual?"

If you're organising, picking the venue gives you the most control over cost. Choosing somewhere with a range of prices on the menu lets people spend what suits them.

## 4. Pay for what you had

This one gets overlooked. On an equal split, the people who order the least pay for everyone else's extras. In a 2016 OpenTable survey, people who felt they lost out on an equal split estimated it at about £8.73 a meal ([here's what that adds up to over a year](/blog/splitting-the-bill-equally-cost/)). That's real money if you're already on a tight social budget.

Agreeing to pay for what you each order means:

- The friend on a budget can have a main and tap water and pay for exactly that.
- Nobody pays for a bottle of wine they didn't drink.
- People don't order more because "it's being split anyway" (a real effect; see [why groups order more when splitting](/blog/why-groups-order-more-when-splitting/)).

## 5. Split the food, pay for your own drinks

Drinks are usually the biggest gap on a bill. If paying by item feels like too much admin, splitting the food equally and having everyone pay for their own drinks fixes most of the unfairness. There's a worked example in [splitting the bill when some people don't drink](/blog/split-bill-when-some-dont-drink/).

## 6. Host at home, properly

A dinner where everyone brings a dish, or a takeaway night where everyone pays for their own order, can cost a fraction of a restaurant. Rotate whose house it is. If you're ordering a takeaway together, sort who owes what straight away (see [how to split a takeaway order](/blog/split-takeaway-order/)).

## 7. Mix in free and cheap plans

Not every catch up needs to involve a bill. Many of the UK's national museums and galleries are free to enter. Walks, park picnics, quiz nights, a coffee instead of drinks, a film night at someone's flat. A group that alternates between a "proper" night out and a cheap one often ends up seeing each other more.

## 8. Give your social spending its own pot

Confused.com's own suggestion is to set a monthly budget for socialising and keep it in a separate savings pot in your banking app. Then you can say yes to plans without wondering whether you can afford it, and no without guilt when the pot's empty.

## 9. Don't let one person carry the bill

If one person pays at the till and waits weeks to get paid back, they end up quietly lending money to the group. Settle up the same night, while everyone remembers what they had. A message with each person's exact amount and a payment link makes it easy:

> "Paid! Priya £19.50, Tom £32, Jess £29, Ali £29. Pay me back when you get a sec."

## If you're the friend who can't afford it

It's more common than you'd think, which makes it easier to say. A few honest lines that don't need an explanation:

> "I can't do dinner this month, but I'd love to come for a drink after."

> "That's a bit out of my budget. Would anyone be up for somewhere cheaper?"

> "I'll come, but I'm just having a main, so I'll pay for mine."

Most friends would rather adjust the plan than not see you.

## FAQ

### How much does a night out cost in the UK?

In an April 2026 Confused.com survey, people said they spend about £62 on average on a restaurant dinner, £58 on a night out or clubbing, and £37 on drinks in a pub or bar. These are self-reported averages, so your area and habits will vary.

### How many people turn down invitations because of cost?

Half of Brits said they'd turned down an invitation because it was too expensive, and 63% of Londoners said they'd had to decline social invitations because of the cost (Confused.com, 2026).

### How can I go out with friends on a budget?

Go for lunch rather than dinner, choose set menus, say your budget before plans are made, and agree to pay for what you each order rather than splitting equally. Mixing in free plans helps too.

### Is it rude to ask to pay for just what I ordered?

No. It's a reasonable request, especially if you mention it before ordering. With so many people watching what they spend, plenty of your friends will be relieved someone said it.
