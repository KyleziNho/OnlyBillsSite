The fair way to split a service charge or tip in a group is in proportion to what each person ordered. If the service charge is 12.5%, each person pays 12.5% of their own food and drink. Discounts work the same way in reverse. Splitting extras equally means the person who ordered least pays the most service per pound spent.

The maths is simple. The fiddly bit is the pennies, which is where most hand-worked splits come out wrong. (Whether you have to pay the service charge at all is a separate question: see [is service charge mandatory in the UK?](/blog/is-service-charge-mandatory-uk/) And for how much to tip in the first place, see [how much to tip in the UK](/blog/how-much-to-tip-uk/).)

## Proportional vs equal: what's the difference?

**Proportional:** each person's share of the extra matches their share of the bill. Order a quarter of the food, pay a quarter of the service charge.

**Equal:** the extra is divided by the number of people, whatever they ordered.

Here's why it matters. Two people share a bill:

| | Alex | Bea | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordered | £10.00 | £50.00 | £60.00 |
| 12.5% service, proportional | £1.25 | £6.25 | £7.50 |
| 12.5% service, equal | £3.75 | £3.75 | £7.50 |

On an equal split, Alex pays £3.75 of service on a £10 order. That's 37.5%. Bea pays 7.5%. Proportional means both pay the same 12.5% rate, which is what the restaurant charged.

A service charge is a percentage of what you spent, so splitting it as a percentage is the fair way.

## A worked example, with the pennies

Four friends have dinner. The restaurant adds a 12.5% service charge to the food and drink, then they use a £10 off voucher.

**Step 1: what each person ordered**

| Person | Ordered |
|---|---|
| Ella | £12.40 |
| Finn | £18.75 |
| Grace | £24.10 |
| Hari | £31.95 |
| **Subtotal** | **£87.20** |

**Step 2: the extras on the bill**

- Service charge: 12.5% of £87.20 = £10.90
- Voucher: minus £10.00
- **Total to pay: £88.10**

**Step 3: spread the extras in proportion**

Each person's share of the extras is their order divided by £87.20.

| Person | Ordered | Service (exact) | Voucher (exact) | Exact share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ella | £12.40 | £1.5500 | minus £1.4220 | £12.5280 |
| Finn | £18.75 | £2.3438 | minus £2.1502 | £18.9435 |
| Grace | £24.10 | £3.0125 | minus £2.7638 | £24.3487 |
| Hari | £31.95 | £3.9938 | minus £3.6640 | £32.2798 |

**Step 4: round once, at the end**

| Person | Pays |
|---|---|
| Ella | £12.53 |
| Finn | £18.94 |
| Grace | £24.35 |
| Hari | £32.28 |
| **Total** | **£88.10** |

That adds up exactly to the bill.

## Where the missing penny comes from

Here's the mistake most people make. They round each piece separately.

Round each person's service charge first: £1.55, £2.34, £3.01, £3.99. Those add up to £10.89, not £10.90. Round each person's voucher share: £1.42, £2.15, £2.76, £3.66. Those add up to £9.99, not £10.00.

You're a penny out on each, and on a bigger table with more extras the gap can grow to several pennies. It sounds trivial, but it's why the person who paid at the till often ends up a few pence short, or why the group chat ends in "hang on, that doesn't add up".

Two ways to avoid it:

1. **Round once, at the end.** Work out each person's exact share including all extras, then round. Check the total.
2. **If you're still a penny out, give it to one person.** The usual convention is the person with the biggest share, or the person with the largest rounding remainder. Nobody will mind a penny, but the total must match the bill.

## The shortcut: one multiplier

Because the service charge and voucher are both spread in proportion, you can do it in one step:

**Multiplier = total to pay ÷ subtotal**

Here that's £88.10 ÷ £87.20 = 1.01032. Multiply each person's order by it:

- Ella: £12.40 × 1.01032 = £12.53
- Finn: £18.75 × 1.01032 = £18.94
- Grace: £24.10 × 1.01032 = £24.35
- Hari: £31.95 × 1.01032 = £32.28

This works for any mix of service charge, tip and discount, as long as they all apply to the whole bill.

## Does it matter much? Equal vs proportional here

| Person | Proportional | Extras split equally | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ella | £12.53 | £12.63 | 10p more |
| Finn | £18.94 | £18.98 | 4p more |
| Grace | £24.35 | £24.32 | 3p less |
| Hari | £32.28 | £32.17 | 11p less |

(Equal: the £10.90 service is £2.725 each and the £10 voucher is £2.50 each, so everyone pays their order plus 22.5p. Rounding those half pennies up gives £88.12, so two people (here Grace and Hari) take a penny off to make £88.10.)

On a table where orders are close, the difference is pennies. It grows with the gap between orders and the size of the extras. For a 15% service charge on a table where one person had £15 and another had £60, an equal split moves about £3.38 from one to the other. Over a year of dinners, that adds up.

## Special cases

**Discounts that only apply to food.** If a "50% off food" deal doesn't cover drinks, spread the discount only across food, in proportion to each person's food. Someone who mostly drank gets less of the discount.

**Service charge on the discounted or full price?** Restaurants differ. Some calculate service before a voucher, some after. Look at the receipt to see what the percentage was applied to, then split what's actually on it. There's a worked example both ways in [how to split a bill with a discount or voucher](/blog/split-bill-with-discount-or-voucher/).

**Tips you add yourselves.** Same rule: in proportion. On a 10% tip, someone who had £20 puts in £2 and someone who had £40 puts in £4.

**Someone wants to tip more.** Let them. Split the agreed tip in proportion, and anyone who wants to add extra can do so on top.

**Shared dishes.** Split a shared dish between the people who had it first, then apply the service charge in proportion to each person's total including their share of the dish. The [shared items, service and tips tutorial](/how-to/shared-items-service-and-tips/) shows how OnlyBills handles this.

## FAQ

### Should service charge be split equally or by what people ordered?

By what people ordered. A service charge is a percentage of the bill, so each person paying that percentage of their own order is the fair way. Equal splitting means lighter eaters pay a higher effective rate.

### How do I split a tip between friends?

Work out each person's share of the bill before the tip, then give each person the same percentage tip on their own share. On a 10% tip, someone who spent £30 adds £3.

### Why don't our shares add up to the bill?

Usually because each piece was rounded separately. Work out each person's exact total first, round once at the end, and give any leftover penny to one person.

### How do you split a discount in a group?

In proportion to what each person spent on the items the discount covers. A £10 off voucher on an £80 meal is 12.5% off everyone's share.
