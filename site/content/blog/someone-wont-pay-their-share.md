If someone hasn't paid their share, start by assuming they forgot, send one clear reminder with the amount and a way to pay, and only then get more direct. Most people pay at the first or second nudge. For the friend who always "forgets", the real fix is changing how you pay on the night, so you're never the one holding their dinner on your card.

It's a common problem. In a 2023 Opinium survey of 2,000 UK adults for Starling Bank, about a third (32%) said they'd had to ask someone two or more times to be paid back, and it took more than two months on average to get the money (Starling Bank, 2023).

:::stats
- 32% | have had to ask someone two or more times to be paid back | Starling Bank / Opinium, 2023
- 17% | started to resent the person who owed them | Starling Bank / Opinium, 2023
- 11% | avoided making plans with them afterwards | Starling Bank / Opinium, 2023
:::

Those last two numbers are the real cost. A £30 dinner isn't usually worth losing a friend over, but quietly resenting someone for months often ends that way anyway.

## First, work out which situation you're in

Not every unpaid share is the same, and the right response depends on which one it is.

| Situation | What's probably going on | Best first move |
|---|---|---|
| They've just not paid yet | Busy, forgot, waiting for the amount | One friendly reminder with a link |
| They disagree with the amount | They think they had less, or didn't drink | Show the receipt, offer to recalculate |
| They can't afford it right now | Money's tight and they're embarrassed | Offer time or instalments, privately |
| They do it every time | A habit, conscious or not | Change how you pay on the night |
| They left without paying | Assumed someone would cover it | Direct message the same evening |

## Step 1: a clear, friendly reminder

Most "won't pay" situations are really "haven't paid yet". The fix is a short message with everything they need to pay without thinking.

> Hey, just a nudge on Friday's dinner, your bit was £32.40. Link's here: [link] 🙏

Put the amount first and include a way to pay. If they have to ask "how much was it again?", you've given them a reason to put it off. Our guide on [how to ask a friend to pay you back](/blog/ask-friend-to-pay-you-back/) has more templates.

## Step 2: a second, slightly more direct reminder

Wait about a week. Keep it short and still warm, but make it a question so they have to reply.

> Hi, sorry to chase again. Still need the £32.40 from Friday. Can you send it this week?

A question with a timeframe gets more replies than a statement. "Can you send it this week?" is polite and clear.

## Step 3: ask what's going on

If there's still nothing, stop chasing the money and ask about the person. There may be a reason.

> Hey, haven't heard back about Friday. Is everything OK? If money's tight right now, honestly no stress, just let me know and we'll sort something.

This often brings out the real issue. They might be skint until payday, think the amount was wrong, or be mortified that they forgot twice. Each of those has an easy answer:

- **Skint:** "Pay me after payday" or "send £10 a week" is fine.
- **Disputes the amount:** send a photo of the receipt and offer to work it out by what everyone had. If they had a lime soda while everyone else drank, they may have a point.
- **Embarrassed:** make it easy. "No worries at all, just whenever you can."

## Step 4: say it face to face

If messages aren't working, mention it in person, briefly and without an audience.

> "Oh, while I think of it, any chance you could send me that £32.40 from Friday's dinner?"

Not in front of the group. Calling someone out in front of friends makes them defensive and usually makes you look worse, even when you're right.

## Step 5: decide whether to let it go

Sometimes the best move is to stop. Let it go if:

- **The amount is small** compared with the friendship. Many people would rather lose £15 than keep chasing.
- **They're genuinely struggling.** Quietly treating them is kind, as long as you're happy with it.
- **Chasing is costing you more stress than the money.**

If you do let it go, actually let it go. Don't bring it up months later. And don't keep covering them, which is where step 6 comes in.

## Step 6: stop it happening again

For a repeat "forgetter", reminders aren't the answer. Changing how the bill gets paid is.

- **Don't be the one who pays.** Let someone else put it on their card, or ask the restaurant to split by card for smaller groups.
- **Sort it before anyone leaves.** "Shall we all send our bits now?" at the table works because everyone's still there and it's the norm.
- **Use one link for the group.** Everyone gets the same message at the same time, so nobody's singled out.
- **Ask for deposits for big plans.** For trips, tickets or stag and hen dos, collect money before you book, not after.

## What if they left without paying?

This one's more direct, because it isn't "forgot to send money", it's "left the table without settling". Message the same evening:

> Hey, think you headed off before we sorted the bill. Your share was £38.10. Can you send it tonight? [link]

Nobody wants to be the person who walked out on the bill, so most people pay quickly once it's pointed out.

## Worked example: when they think they owe less

Five friends, a bill of £187.50 including 12.5% service. Split equally, it's £37.50 each. Jo had a main and a soft drink, a £20 subtotal, and says £37.50 isn't fair.

She's right. With the 12.5% service added, her order comes to £22.50. Splitting by what everyone had, the other four cover the remaining £165.00 between them based on what they ordered. Jo pays £22.50, sends it straight away, and the friendship is fine. Sometimes "won't pay" means "won't pay that".

## FAQ

### What do you do when a friend never pays you back?
Send one clear reminder with the amount and a link, then a second after a week. If that doesn't work, ask privately if everything's OK. For a repeat offender, stop paying for them on the night and get everyone to pay before they leave.

### How many times should you ask someone to pay you back?
Usually twice by message and once in person is reasonable. After that, decide whether the amount is worth more than the stress, and change how you pay with that person in future.

### Is it petty to chase a friend for money?
No. If you agreed to split a bill and paid for them, asking for their share is fair. What matters is how you ask: privately, with the exact amount, and without making it a big deal.

### Can you take a friend to small claims court for an unpaid bill?
In theory you can claim money owed, but for a shared dinner it's rarely worth it. A direct conversation is almost always better, and for small amounts, letting it go and changing how you pay next time protects both your money and the friendship.
