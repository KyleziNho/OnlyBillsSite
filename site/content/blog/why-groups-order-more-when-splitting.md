When a group agrees to split the bill equally, people order more. In a restaurant experiment by Gneezy, Haruvy and Yafe (2004), diners who knew the bill would be split spent about 36% more than diners paying for their own food, and most of them said they'd rather have paid individually.

Economists call this the diner's dilemma. It's a neat explanation for why "let's just split it" dinners so often end up pricier than anyone expected.

:::stats
- 37 → 51 | average shekels spent per person: paying for yourself vs splitting equally | Gneezy et al., 2004
- 82 | average shekels spent when the meal was free | Gneezy et al., 2004
- 80% | of those asked (19 of 24) would rather pay for what they ordered | Gneezy et al., 2004
:::

## What is the diner's dilemma?

Imagine six of you are splitting the bill equally. You're deciding whether to add a £6 dessert.

If you were paying for yourself, the dessert costs you £6. On an equal split, it adds £6 to the bill, but that £6 is shared six ways. So your dessert costs you £1, and your five friends pay the other £5.

Seen that way, the dessert is a bargain. The trouble is that everyone at the table has the same bargain on everything they order. If all six people add a £6 dessert, each person pays £6 more, which is exactly what it would have cost them on their own. Nobody saved anything, and the bill went up by £36.

Tim Harford wrote about this in his Undercover Economist column ("Free-riding à la carte", 2013), using the restaurant experiment below as his example.

That's the dilemma. Each person's sensible choice, added up, gives a result most of them didn't want.

## The restaurant experiment, in plain English

Uri Gneezy, Ernan Haruvy and Hadas Yafe wanted to see whether this happens with real food, not just in theory. Their paper, "The Inefficiency of Splitting the Bill", was published in the Economic Journal in 2004.

**The setup**

- Students were recruited from the Technion campus in Haifa, Israel, and invited to lunch at a popular restaurant nearby. 60 took part in the three main rules below, and 12 more in the fourth.
- Each group was six people (three men, three women) who didn't know each other.
- Everyone was paid 80 shekels (about $20 at the time) just for turning up.
- They wrote down their orders privately, in silence, so nobody could influence anyone else.

**The rules**

Each group was told one of these before ordering:

1. **Pay for yourself.** You pay for exactly what you order.
2. **Split equally.** The bill is divided evenly between the six of you.
3. **Free.** The researchers pay for everything.
4. **Pay a sixth of your own meal.** You pay one sixth of what you order and the researchers cover the rest.

**What happened**

| Rule | Average spent per person |
|---|---|
| Pay for yourself | 37.3 shekels |
| Split equally | 50.9 shekels |
| You pay a sixth of your own meal | 57.4 shekels |
| Free | 82.3 shekels |

Moving from "pay for yourself" to "split equally" pushed spending up from about 37 to about 51 shekels, about 36% more. When the meal was free, people spent more than twice as much.

## The clever bit: the fourth rule

The fourth rule is what makes the study convincing.

Under an equal split, ordering an extra 6 shekels of food costs you 1 shekel and costs your friends 5. Under the "pay a sixth" rule, ordering an extra 6 shekels still costs you just 1 shekel, but costs your friends nothing (the researchers pay).

If people were holding back to be kind to their tablemates, they should have ordered noticeably more under the fourth rule, because nobody else gets hurt. They spent a little more (57.4 shekels against 50.9), but the difference wasn't statistically significant. The authors point out that the fourth group was small, so this part is suggestive rather than proof.

In other words, diners didn't seem to be thinking much about the others at the table. They were reacting to what an extra dish cost them. Make the extra dish cheap for the person ordering, and they order more.

## Most people didn't want to split

Before learning which rule they'd get, some diners were asked how they'd prefer to pay. 19 of the 24 asked (80%) said they'd rather pay for what they ordered.

So most people preferred to pay individually. But once they were told the bill would be split, they behaved in a way that made the split more expensive for everyone. The paper's point is that this isn't greed. It's people responding sensibly to the rules they've been given.

## How much should you trust this?

It's a good study with real limits, so it's worth being honest about them:

- **It was small.** 24 diners for each of the first two rules, 12 for the free meal and 12 for the fourth rule.
- **They were strangers.** Friends who eat together often may hold back more, because they'll see each other again. The authors say as much.
- **It was students in Israel in the early 2000s.** Prices, menus and social norms differ.

So don't quote "36%" as a law of nature. The direction is what matters, and it lines up with what most people have seen at a big birthday dinner.

## What this means for your next dinner

You don't need to ban splitting. You just need to stop the rules from nudging everyone to order more than they want.

**Agree how you'll pay before anyone orders.** The effect happens at the moment of ordering. Deciding afterwards is too late. (It's rule one in our [bill splitting etiquette](/blog/bill-splitting-etiquette/) for this reason.)

> "Shall we each pay for what we have tonight? Makes it easier."

**Pay for what you had.** This is the only rule in the experiment where nobody is nudged to overorder, because every extra dish costs you its full price.

**Split shared things between the people who shared them.** A bottle of wine for three is split three ways, not six. The side everyone picked at is split across everyone.

**Watch big tables most.** The effect gets stronger as the group grows. At a table of ten splitting equally, a £10 cocktail costs you £1. That's where bills run away. See our guide to [splitting the bill with a large group](/blog/split-bill-large-group/).

**Keep the maths off the table.** The usual reason people split equally is that itemising is a faff. If one person can scan the receipt and send each person their number, the main argument for splitting goes away.

## FAQ

### What is the unscrupulous diner's dilemma?

It's the idea that when a group splits a bill equally, each person pays only a fraction of anything extra they order, so everyone orders more and the total goes up. It's a classic example of free-riding.

### Do people really spend more when splitting the bill?

In Gneezy, Haruvy and Yafe's 2004 restaurant experiment, they did: about 51 shekels each when splitting versus about 37 when paying individually. The study was small (groups of six students in Israel), so the exact size of the effect may differ elsewhere.

### Is it better to split the bill or pay individually?

Paying for what you had avoids the overordering effect and is fairer when people order differently. Splitting equally is fine when everyone had roughly the same, or when you genuinely shared everything.

### Does this happen with friends as well as strangers?

Probably less, because friends notice and care about each other. But the incentive is the same, so some of it still happens, especially at big tables.
